Housing stability / New York City

A stable home.
A school to keep.

A school offers continuity. Housing helps determine whether a student can hold on to it.

More than 154,000 NYC public school students—nearly one in seven—experienced homelessness in 2024–25. Nearly 65,000 spent time in City shelters; more than 82,000 temporarily shared someone else’s housing. [1]

Here, homelessness includes temporarily sharing housing because of housing loss or economic hardship, as well as shelters and other unstable sleeping arrangements.

“Without stable shelter, everything else falls apart.”— Matthew Desmond · interview

01 / Before the headline

Start with
the school districts.

The map begins in 2021–22. Each circle represents the number of students identified as homeless by a geographic-district reporting agency. Larger circles mean larger reported counts.

District 24 in Queens reported 7,388 students. [2]

These records describe reporting agencies, not student residences. The map uses 2026 reference boundaries; historical consistency has not been established.

02 / Two school years later

The same city.
Larger reported counts.

By 2023–24, all 32 reporting districts recorded an increase. The circles use the same size scale as before.

District 24 reported 11,702 students, compared with 7,388 two years earlier. The records do not explain how much of the change reflects housing conditions, enrollment, student movement, or identification. [2]

The opening’s citywide headline is from 2024–25. These district maps cover the earlier 2021–24 workbook.

03 / A closer look

East Harlem.
District 4.

4,351students reported in 2023–24

The district reported 1,961 students in 2021–22, 3,045 in 2022–23, and 4,351 in 2023–24—an increase of about 122% from the first year. [2]

Housing instability also places pressure on attendance. A separate citywide AFC brief reports 2022–23 chronic absence of 67% for students in shelter, compared with 34% for permanently housed students. These are separate findings, not outcomes calculated from the district map. [3]

04 / Beyond the school gates

College begins.
Housing uncertainty can remain.

CUNY’s published 2024 survey estimate is 5% reporting homelessness in the past 12 months. The map shows institution locations, with campus figures available for 19 of the 26 records. [4]

At Queensborough, the published figure is 4%; at LaGuardia, 5%. These are weighted survey estimates, not administrative counts. Small differences should not be read as campus rankings.

K–12 and college data describe different student populations; they do not follow the same people over time. Hollow points have no separate undergraduate survey result. Points show institutions, not student homes.

05 / Educational continuity

An address changes.
A school can remain.

A classroom offers a place to return to. Housing and transportation help determine whether a student can get there.

NYC Public Schools describes enrollment, school-continuity, and transportation support. CUNY resource centers describe housing referrals and basic-needs assistance. These resources do not establish current capacity for every student.

When a student’s address changes, how can the city help preserve the continuity of their education?

That is the question this map begins to ask. Understanding the barriers requires listening to students, families, and the people who support them.
