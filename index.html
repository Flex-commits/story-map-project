Housing stability / New York City

A project by Thomas Hagos

A stable home.A school to keep.

A school offers continuity. Housing helps determine whether a student can hold on to it.

During the 2024–25 school year, more than 154,000 New York City public school students, nearly one in seven, experienced homelessness. Nearly 65,000 spent time in City shelters, while more than 82,000 temporarily shared someone else’s housing. These figures place housing stability at the center of a question usually asked of schools: what conditions allow a child to learn?

Housing stability shapes everyday life. Without a dependable place to return to, keeping a job, managing an illness, or caring for family can require constant negotiation. Much of that uncertainty is difficult to see: a borrowed room, a temporary stay extended another week, a journey that begins somewhere different each morning. Housing provides a base from which people can make commitments and plan beyond the immediate demands of the day.

For children, those commitments include going to school. The school calendar assumes continuity even when a family’s living arrangements offer none. A student may still have a classroom, a teacher, and an assigned desk, yet face a longer journey, disrupted sleep, or nowhere quiet to study. Enrollment secures a place in the education system; housing helps make that place accessible in practice. This is where the broader question of homelessness becomes a question of educational opportunity: how much stability does a child need outside the classroom to benefit from what happens inside it?

This project examines where student homelessness is concentrated across New York City and considers what those patterns mean for the coordination of housing, schools, and transportation. Maps cannot explain every student’s circumstances, but they can help identify where the responsibility for educational continuity extends beyond the classroom.

When a student’s address changes, how can the city help preserve the continuity of their education?

01 / What is being counted

A roof is not always
a stable home.

For school students, homelessness concerns whether their nighttime residence is fixed, regular, and adequate. It includes sharing someone else’s housing because of housing loss or economic hardship, living in a shelter or hotel, and sleeping in places not meant as a home.

This does not mean everyone staying with relatives is homeless. The circumstances and adequacy of the arrangement matter.

NYC Public Schools: students in temporary housing ↗

The college figures later in this story come from survey answers about the past 12 months. They are not school homelessness classifications or a count taken on one night.

What the reporting rules establish

The NYSED SIRS manual describes homeless program code 8262 and reporting of primary nighttime residence. The workbook counts students identified by each LEA during a year; transfers can lead to appearances in more than one agency’s records.

06 / What the consequences look like

Getting to class
is part of the story.

The map now shows AFC’s published percentage ranges. Unlike the workbook counts, this analysis uses cumulative enrollment and includes charter pupils; District 75 is excluded from district denominators. I have not calculated exact rates from these ranges.

A separate AFC brief reports 2022–23 chronic absenteeism of 67% for students in shelter and 34% for permanently housed students. Chronic absence means missing at least one in ten school days.

For 2023, the same brief reports four-year graduation rates of 63% for students in shelter and 85% for permanently housed students. Its educational indicators concern NYCPS students and exclude charter schools.

These are separately published citywide findings, not outcomes calculated from my district workbook. They show an association, not proof of what caused it.

08 / Queensborough · Read the evidence

One campus.
More than one kind of need.

4%reported homelessness
38%campus-report four-item composite

Queensborough’s campus report gives 4% for homelessness and 38% for a four-item housing insecurity composite. The central CUNY dashboard also gives 4% for homelessness, but 44% on its housing insecurity scale. The campus map uses the central dashboard throughout; the 38% here is a separate campus-report measure.

The spring 2024 survey asked about homelessness in the past 12 months. Queensborough’s 38% composite includes a yes to any of four items: a difficult rent increase, unpaid rent, unpaid utilities, or homelessness. It includes the homelessness item; the two percentages must not be added. There were 474 total respondents and a 12% response rate. CUNY’s central methods describe weighting and suppression of results with fewer than 25 respondents. Item sample sizes vary; I have not established confidence intervals or significance tests.

LaGuardia’s separate report gives a 5% homelessness figure, with 747 respondents and a 14.9% response rate. These small differences should not be read as a campus ranking. Its rent-difficulty result differs by one percentage point: 39% in the central dashboard, 40% in the campus report. I use 39% in the map and record the difference below.

09 / A published account · September 2026

Someone’s education.
Someone’s daily life.

Bronx Community College published Christopher Thomas’s account of studying while living in a shelter. When he enrolled in 2020, he faced a long commute; the switch to remote classes and unreliable access to technology contributed to his decision to step away.

The college reports that he later returned, found support through family and the campus community, and was accepted to Columbia. His account makes the pressures concrete. It cannot tell us how common that experience is.

10 / From evidence to investigation

What help exists?
What still gets in the way?

The data establish a need. They do not yet establish the right product.

I want to understand what families and college students have to do to find housing support, and where they get stuck. The numbers give me a starting point. My next step is to learn from students, families, and the people who work with them.

NYCPS describes enrollment, school-continuity, and transportation support. CUNY resource centers describe housing referrals and other basic-needs assistance. These links establish that support is described, not that a place, appointment, or funding is available to every student.

  • Which steps leave students or families waiting without a clear next action?
  • Which barriers involve information or follow-up, and which involve housing supply, eligibility, or funding?
  • What do students and staff say would help most?

That is where I want to take HOMES OS next: investigate one specific barrier with students and staff before deciding whether a tool would help. I am not claiming that a platform would solve homelessness.

These are research questions. This workbook does not establish that service coordination failures caused student homelessness.

The descriptive findings

What the evidence shows.

All 32 reporting districts recorded an increase between 2021–22 and 2023–24. The median district increase was 42.1%; district changes ranged from 10.9% to 131.1%. This describes changes in reported counts, not changes in the probability that a student experiences homelessness. [01]

CUNY’s published university-weighted 2024 result is 5% reporting homelessness in the past 12 months and 49% on its housing insecurity scale. The published campus homelessness results range from 1% to 11% across 19 campuses. These are whole-percent survey estimates. The campus range is not a confidence interval or evidence of statistically significant differences. [04]

The university result comes from CUNY’s own weighting. It is not an average of the campus percentages. School counts and college percentages are never added together.

Notes on the analysis and what changed
  1. Check the original. I compared the published SIRS workbook with my supplied file: identical SHA-256 hashes and zero differences across 10,530 cells. I retained the 32 geographic-district LEA records and kept their identifiers. Separate charter records, District 75, and the central office are excluded from this count layer.
  2. Calculate change, not a new prevalence rate. For each district: (2023–24 count − 2021–22 count) ÷ 2021–22 count × 100. I calculated the median and range from the unrounded changes, then rounded those summaries to one decimal. The median gives each district equal weight; it is not a citywide student growth rate. Transfers can duplicate students across LEAs, so I did not sum the districts into a unique city total.
  3. Retrieve the right college results. I selected 2024, the Basic Needs section, and each college sector in CUNY’s dashboard; I checked those filters in the visible dashboard. I extracted published whole-percent answers for homelessness, rent difficulty, rent underpayment, and the housing insecurity scale. This expands coverage from two campus reports to 19 campus profiles.
  4. Record disagreements. Queensborough’s central housing scale is 44%; its campus report gives 38% for a four-item composite. I show the 38% separately in its chapter. The central scale’s exact scoring is not established here. LaGuardia’s rent difficulty is 39% centrally versus 40% in its report; the reason is unresolved. Campus comparisons consistently use the central dashboard.
  5. Keep uncertainty visible. Seven institution records have no separate column in this undergraduate export. They remain gray, with a specific explanation. I did not fill them with averages, convert weighted counts into sample sizes, estimate confidence intervals without survey-design information, or turn percentages into student headcounts. CUNY reports 7,035 completed 2024 surveys and an 8.6% overall response rate; item samples vary and nonresponse can affect results.
