01 / What is being counted

A roof is not always
a stable home.

For school students, homelessness concerns whether their nighttime residence is fixed, regular, and adequate. It includes sharing someone else’s housing because of housing loss or economic hardship, living in a shelter or hotel, and sleeping in places not meant as a home.

This does not mean everyone staying with relatives is homeless. The circumstances and adequacy of the arrangement matter.

NYC Public Schools: students in temporary housing ↗

The college figures later in this story come from survey answers about the past 12 months. They are not school homelessness classifications or a count taken on one night.

What the reporting rules establish

The NYSED SIRS manual describes homeless program code 8262 and reporting of primary nighttime residence. The workbook counts students identified by each LEA during a year; transfers can lead to appearances in more than one agency’s records.

A different lens.

District 30 in Queens rose from 3,684 to 8,512, about a 131% increase. A fast-growing count is a signal to investigate, not an explanation of what happened.

A 131% increase describes a change in an agency count. It is not the proportion of enrolled students experiencing homelessness.

AFC’s separate 2023–24 analysis uses cumulative enrollment and includes charter pupils. It publishes ranges, not exact district percentages. The interactive atlas offers this as a separate measure.

District 4 reported 1,961 students in 2021–22, 3,045 in 2022–23, and 4,351 in 2023–24—an increase of about 122% from the first year.

These records make the district a useful place to begin asking questions about family housing stability. They do not show individual families’ housing journeys or whether they received support.

06 / What the consequences look like

Getting to class
is part of the story.

The map now shows AFC’s published percentage ranges. Unlike the workbook counts, this analysis uses cumulative enrollment and includes charter pupils; District 75 is excluded from district denominators. I have not calculated exact rates from these ranges.

A separate AFC brief reports 2022–23 chronic absenteeism of 67% for students in shelter and 34% for permanently housed students. Chronic absence means missing at least one in ten school days.

For 2023, the same brief reports four-year graduation rates of 63% for students in shelter and 85% for permanently housed students. Its educational indicators concern NYCPS students and exclude charter schools.

These are separately published citywide findings, not outcomes calculated from my district workbook. They show an association, not proof of what caused it.

08 / Queensborough · Read the evidence

One campus.
More than one kind of need.

4%reported homelessness
38%reported housing insecurity

Queensborough’s 2024 report distinguishes a homelessness question from the broader housing insecurity measure. Both describe survey experience, not a headcount of students living on campus.

The spring 2024 survey asked about homelessness in the past 12 months. Queensborough’s 38% composite includes a yes to any of four items: a difficult rent increase, unpaid rent, unpaid utilities, or homelessness. It includes the homelessness item; the two percentages must not be added. There were 474 total respondents and a 12% response rate. CUNY’s central methods describe weighting and suppression of results with fewer than 25 respondents. Item sample sizes vary; I have not established confidence intervals or significance tests.

LaGuardia’s separate report gives a 5% homelessness figure, with 747 respondents and a 14.9% response rate. These small differences should not be read as a campus ranking.

09 / A published account · September 2026

Someone’s education.
Someone’s daily life.

Bronx Community College published Christopher Thomas’s account of studying while living in a shelter. When he enrolled in 2020, he faced a long commute; the switch to remote classes and unreliable access to technology contributed to his decision to step away.

The college reports that he later returned, found support through family and the campus community, and was accepted to Columbia. His account makes the pressures concrete. It cannot tell us how common that experience is.

Read BCC’s published account, September 10, 2026 ↗

I did not interview Christopher. This is a later published account, not a participant linked to the 2021–24 school data or a measured outcome of a housing program.

10 / From evidence to investigation

What help exists?
What still gets in the way?

The data establish a need. They do not yet establish the right product.

I want to understand what families and college students have to do to find housing support, and where they get stuck. The numbers give me a starting point. My next step is to learn from students, families, and the people who work with them.

NYCPS describes enrollment, school-continuity, and transportation support. CUNY resource centers describe housing referrals and other basic-needs assistance. These links establish that support is described, not that a place, appointment, or funding is available to every student.

  • Which steps leave students or families waiting without a clear next action?
  • Which barriers involve information or follow-up, and which involve housing supply, eligibility, or funding?
  • What do students and staff say would help most?

That is where I want to take HOMES OS next: investigate one specific barrier with students and staff before deciding whether a tool would help. I am not claiming that a platform would solve homelessness.

These are research questions. This workbook does not establish that service coordination failures caused student homelessness.

11 / Data and sources

Where the
numbers come from.

School district counts: I used the NYSED SIRS workbook, SED-SIRS-3-Year-Summary-2022-2024.xlsx, “3-yr Homeless Counts” worksheet. NYS-TEACHS publishes the original download. I compared the published file with my supplied copy: identical SHA-256 hashes and no differences across 10,530 worksheet cells. View the comparison record.

Boundaries: NYC Department of City Planning, School Districts, release 26b, downloaded October 24, 2026. These are 2026 reference boundaries. Historical archive access failed; stability over 2021–24 is not established. Read the boundary review.

The reporting-LEA count layer excludes separate charter records, District 75, and the central office. Students can be counted by multiple education agencies. Do not sum these records into a unique citywide total or add years into a count of unique people.

Suppressed values are treated as unavailable, never zero. The workbook view shows agency counts and count changes. AFC’s separate view shows published enrollment-based ranges, not exact calculated district rates. Neither shows student home locations.

College locations: New York State, CUNY University Campus Locations, downloaded October 24, 2026. I included all 26 institution locations in this file. SUNY and private universities are not included yet.

College surveys: Queensborough 2024 and LaGuardia 2024. Two campus reports have verified figures; five other campus source pages have been reviewed without establishing comparable figures. The remaining campus reports have not yet been checked. Homelessness, housing insecurity, and difficulty paying rent are different measures that can overlap. I keep them separate and do not turn the survey percentages into student counts.

Support links: I included campus support pages so readers can learn more. I have not confirmed current availability or eligibility. The map does not identify students or show where they live.

Review records: Evidence audit and remaining questions · Interview questions and consent approach. No interviews or external outreach have been conducted for this project.
